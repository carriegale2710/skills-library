# Build Habit

Turn an intention into a system small enough to run on a bad day. Goals give direction; systems create progress. People slip to the level of their systems, so design the system, not more motivation. Output one habit card, not an essay.

## Step 1: Pin down the habit

- **Behaviour, not outcome.** "Write 1 page", not "finish the book". If the user gives a goal, ask what repeated action would produce it.
- **Build or break?**
- **Identity.** "I'm someone who ___." Each repetition is a vote for that identity, which is what makes the habit feel natural rather than forced.
- **Anchor.** Something they already do every day at the right time and place (coffee, commute, shutting the laptop, school drop-off).

Ask only for what's missing, in one short question (multiple choice if a question tool is available). If several habits are listed, pick the keystone one and park the rest: one habit at a time.

## Step 2: Map the loop

Cue → craving → response → reward. Understand the loop to change it.

- **Building:** choose a cue that reliably exists in their day.
- **Breaking:** name the current loop: what triggers it and what it gives them (relief, distraction, connection). Keep the reward, swap the response. Replacing beats removing.

## Step 3: Apply the four laws

Pick one lever per law that fits their real life. Not all of them.

| Law | Build: make it... | Break: make it... | Levers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cue | Obvious | Invisible | Habit stack "After I [current habit], I will [new habit]"; "I will [X] at [time] in [place]"; put the cue in sight; hide the bad cue |
| Craving | Attractive | Unattractive | Pair it with something enjoyed; join people for whom it's normal; reframe "have to" as "get to" |
| Response | Easy | Difficult | 2-minute version; prep ahead; remove steps for the good habit; add steps for the bad one (log out, leave it at the shop) |
| Reward | Satisfying | Unsatisfying | Immediate small reward; tick a tracker; accountability partner or a small cost for skipping |

**Easy matters most.** If they change only one thing, lower the effort to start the good habit and raise it for the bad one.

## Step 4: Shrink to start

The 2-minute version is the habit's opening move: "put on running shoes", "open the doc and write one sentence", "send one outreach message", "open the banking app". Master showing up before optimising. Consistency beats intensity.

## Step 5: Plan for slips

- **Bad-day minimum:** the smallest version that still counts.
- **Never miss twice.** One miss is an accident; two starts a new habit.
- **Expect lag.** 1% better a day compounds, but results show up late. For the first weeks, judge by reps done, not results.

## Output

```
**Habit:** ...
**Identity:** I'm someone who ...
**Obvious:** After I ..., I will ... (or: at [time] in [place])
**Attractive:** ...
**Easy:** 2-minute version: ... | Friction removed: ...
**Satisfying:** ...
**Bad-day minimum:** ...
**First rep:** [next time the cue happens]
```

For breaking a habit, use the same card with the inverted laws and a **Replacement response** line.

End with "Next action: ..." naming one setup step that takes 5 minutes or less (shoes by the door, app moved off the home screen, tracker on the fridge).

## Example

"I want to practise guitar but never do." → Habit: play 1 song. Identity: I'm a musician. Obvious: after I make dinner, guitar stays on its stand by the couch. Attractive: play songs I love, not drills. Easy: 2-minute version is tune up and play one chord progression. Satisfying: tick the calendar. Bad-day minimum: tune up. Next action: move the guitar out of its case onto a stand by the couch.

## Rules

- Environment beats willpower. Don't lecture about discipline or moralise about slips.
- Fit their actual routine and constraints (shifts, kids, energy); ask if unknown.
- Getting stuck on a single task rather than a repeating one → get-unstuck.
- Don't create calendar events, reminders or trackers in external tools unless asked.
